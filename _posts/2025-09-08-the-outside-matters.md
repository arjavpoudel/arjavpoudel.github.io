_Lessons from Christmas cards and Apple Packaging_

Back in primary school, we had a quaint little custom of writing Christmas cards for the entire class (not just the teacher, brown-nosing wasn't really a thing yet). It was a gesture, and any seven-year-old trudging home with a fat stack of seasons greetings from their peers was a heartwarming sight for any parent. 

There were 30 people in my class. By card 7, I was drumming my fingers on the table. By card 15, I was thinking about dinner. By card 23, my eyelids were getting heavy. And by card 29, I was mentally scoring _Eye of the Tiger_. To make matters worse, I'd just discovered the art of "bubble writing." So in addition to writing a custom message for each card (something my parents insisted was "nice and personal"), even the envelopes bore the recipient's name in big, fancy bubble letters.

My mum spotted the pile on my desk and questioned why on earth I was bubble-writing on envelopes - the little white slips designed to be torn up and tossed. To paraphrase "Why spend so much time on something bound for the bin?"

Her reasoning made sense. Even as a seven-year-old, the futility wasn't lost on me: I was investing time into something disposable. Time I could be spending on the contents. Or Mariokart. 

Lesson learned: Who cares about the packaging? One throws away the packaging. People hold onto the inside, so focus on that. 

At 14, I unboxed my first smartphone--an iPhone 7+. The presentation was meticulous. Intricate. Layered. Peeling back the structure was a nice end-to-end experience.  At 18, I got a MacBook for university (you can really see the apple fanboy rhetoric take shape). Once again, the unboxing was a delight. So much so that I didn't even throw those boxes away.

So naturally I found myself coming to blows with the childhood epiphany.  

Why does a multi-trillion dollar company like Apple give a shit about the way their containers look and feel - wasn't the perfect  geometry, smooth sliding lids, matryoshka-doll compartments a means to an end!  Clearly the design team had never known the pain of handwritten Christmas cards. Surely the product itself was enough?

Paul Graham has an essay called _Do Things That Don't Scale_, where he talks about the disproportionate payoff from seemingly tiny details. Apple is the poster child: Jobs famously obsessed over typography after a random college class, and decades later, it gave their products a level of polish that made the competition look like they were written in Comic Sans. In his biography, Jobs even argued that "people do judge the little things". Apple's engineers even agonise over  the _sound_ a MacBook lid makes when it shuts. Steven Bartlett makes the same point in _The Diary of a CEO_, in chapter called 'Sweat the small stuff': micro-experiences add up in non-trivial ways to boost user experience. Individually, these details seem neurotic. Collectively, they create magic.

Packaging is emotive. Presentation shapes perception. And I don't just mean products. If  each passer-by could instantly see through our individual muddied exteriors to the gem within, appearances would be irrelevant. But they can't. So they aren't. 

By now we've all been force-fed the maxim: "Never judge a book by its cover — it's only the inside that counts". How holier than thou of you. You're conflating an ideal society (one that doesn't care) with a real one (one that shouldn't care, but very much does). But by all means, teach them that we only care about the 'inside', then act shocked when people laugh at their forehead tattoo.

This is a false dilemma, a black and white deal. Yes, While judging _only_ by appearances is shallow, to dismiss appearances entirely is naive. The very notion of 'outside' arises because one needed a term for 'not inside'.  The very concept only exists because it serves a purpose. Yes, we should bias toward the inside - [[Asymmetrical Upshot]] - but know that neither extreme serves us well.

Meeting someone new is a case in point. We don't get instant access to their character; that's slow, messy, and uncertain. On a first date, if one person scrubs up well and the other rocks up looking like laundry day, is anyone's gut reaction_"It's okay, it's what's inside that counts"_? (If there are people that think this way, I'd love to put you in an FMRI machine sometime). More likely, it's: _"They couldn't even be bothered to put on a clean shirt."_ Extenuating circumstances (delayed flights, zombie apocalypses) aside, lack of effort in presentation signals lack of investment in the interaction.

Effort signals investment. That's the whole point. If you don't believe me, ask the peacocks. 

Now, the bar is relative. If someone typically drifts through life in "no-fucks-given" attire but decides to put on cologne or a bit of makeup, that's already meaningful. You could argue that, since you don't necessarily know their baseline, you shouldn't judge too quickly. Fair. But humans are wired to notice effort when it's visible. Visibility matters. What gets measured gets managed.  if you want to manage how you're perceived, or for people to notice you care, you'd better make it easy to measure.

Impressions are important. Apple's welcome box isn't just cardboard--it's a ritual.  Disney obsesses over bin placement, ensuring you're never more than 30 steps from a trash can. Airbnb isn't just a place to crash for the night; the best ones leave welcome notes and local treats because they know arrival sets the tone. Certain Japanese companies employ people whose entire job is to bow to customers--pure costly signalling that says "we value you enough to dedicate human resources to your dignity". Dressing nicer than usual, being overtly warm and kind--this is all signalling. Whether it's a company's first impression, or a person's , when there's already so little signal to go on, it doesn't hurt to give your image a leg-up. David Perell has a hotel bathroom principle: Always dress well enough to walk into a bathroom at a hotel you're not staying at and get away with it.


So yes: my seven-year-old self was right that the inside matters more. But my 18-year-old self was right too: the outside isn't irrelevant. Packaging matters. Presentation matters. Appearances matter.

The outside matters.
