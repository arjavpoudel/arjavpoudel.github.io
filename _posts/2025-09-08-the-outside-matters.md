_Lessons from Christmas cards and Apple Packaging_

Back in primary school, we had a quaint little custom of writing Christmas cards for the entire class (not just the teacher, brown-nosing wasn't really a thing yet). It was a gesture - you can imagine a seven-year-old trudging home with a fat stack of seasons greetings from their peers was a heartwarming sight for any parent. 

There were 30 people in my class. By card 7, I was drumming my fingers on the table. By card 15, I was wondering what was for dinner. By card 23, my eyelids were getting heavy. And by card 29, I was mentally scoring _Eye of the Tiger_. To make matters worse, I'd just discovered the art of bubble writing. So in addition to writing a custom message for each card (something my parents insisted was "nice and personal"), the envelopes bore the recipient's name in big, balloon-like letters.

My mum spotted the pile on my desk and questioned why on earth I was bubble-writing on envelopes - the little white slips designed to be torn up and tossed. Why spend so much time on something bound for the bin?

Her reasoning made sense. Even back then, the futility wasn't lost on me: I was investing time into something disposable. Time I could be spending on the contents. Or Mariokart. 

Lesson learned: Who cares about the packaging? One throws away the packaging. People hold onto the inside, so focus on that. 

At 14, I unboxed my first smartphone--an iPhone 7+. The presentation was meticulous. Peeling back the layers was a nice end-to-end experience. I later got a MacBook for university (you can really see the apple fanboy rhetoric unfold in real-time). Once again, the unboxing ritual was a delight. So much so that I didn't even throw those boxes away.

Naturally I found myself coming to blows with the childhood epiphany.  

Why does a multi-trillion dollar company like Apple give a shit about the way their containers look and feel - weren't the smooth sliding lids and matryoshka-doll compartments simply a means to an end?! Clearly the design team had never known the straits of handwritten Christmas cards. Surely the product itself was enough?

Paul Graham has an essay titled _Do Things That Don't Scale_, where he discusses the disproportionate payoff that comes from attention to miniature detail. Apple is the poster child: Jobs famously obsessed over typography after a random college class, and decades later, it gave their products a level of polish that made the competition look like they were written in Comic Sans. In his biography, he argued that "people do judge the little things" (Apple's engineers even agonise over the _sound_ a MacBook lid makes when it shuts). Steven Bartlett makes the same point in _The Diary of a CEO_, in chapter called 'Sweat the small stuff': micro-experiences add up in non-trivial ways to boost user experience. Individually, these details feel neurotic. Collectively, they create magic.

Packaging is emotive. Presentation shapes perception. And I don't just mean products. If each passer-by could instantly see beyond our muddied exteriors to the gem within, appearances would be irrelevant. But they can't. So they aren't. 

By now we've all been force-fed the maxim: "Never judge a book by its cover — it's only the inside that counts". How holier than thou of you. You're conflating an ideal society (one that doesn't care how things look) with a real one (one that shouldn't care, yet does anyway). But by all means, teach them that we only care about the 'inside', then act shocked when people laugh at their forehead tattoo.

This is a crude binarisation. Yes, While judging _only_ by appearances is shallow, to dismiss appearances entirely is naive. The very notion of “outside” exists because we needed a word for “not inside” — the concept only makes sense relative to what it excludes. Sure, we should have a bias toward the inside - [[Asymmetrical Upshot]] - but neither extreme serves us well.

Meeting someone new is case in point. We don't have on-demand access to their character; it's a slow, awkward, altogether unreliable affair. On a first date, if one person scrubs up well and the other rolls in looking like laundry day, who's really thinking _"It's okay, it's what's on the inside that counts"_? (If you are, I'd love to put you in an FMRI machine stat). For us mere mortals, extenuating circumstances (delayed flights, zombie apocalypses) notwithstanding, lack of effort in presentation signals lack of investment in the interaction.

Effort signals investment. That's the whole point. If you don't believe me, ask the peacocks. 

Now, the bar is relative. If someone who typically drifts through life in "no-fucks-given" attire shows up with aftershave or a touch of makeup, that's meaningful already. You could argue that, since you don't know their baseline, you shouldn't judge too quickly. Fair. But humans are wired to notice effort when it's visible. What gets measured gets managed. If you want to manage how you're perceived, or for people to notice you care, you'd better make it easy to measure. 

 David Perell’s “hotel bathroom principle” says it best: dress well enough to stroll into the bathroom of a hotel you’re not staying at, no one blinks twice.

Impressions matter. Apple's welcome box isn't just cardboard - it's theatre. Disneyland places bins so you’re never more than 30 steps from one. Airbnb isn't just a place to crash for the night; the best ones leave welcome notes and local treats because they know arrival sets the tone. In Japan, some companies pay people just to bow to their clinets — “we value you enough to devote precious human resources on your dignity". Dressing sharper than usual, being warmer than expected - this is all signalling. Whether it's a company's first impression, or a person's, when there's already so little signal to go on, it doesn't hurt to give your image a leg-up. 

So yes: my seven-year-old self was right that the inside matters more. But my university-bound self was right too: The outside matters.
