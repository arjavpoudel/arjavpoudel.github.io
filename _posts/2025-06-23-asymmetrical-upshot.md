Common wisdom says extremes are bad - we are to find the balance, the golden mean. True in the binary sense -  most benefits don't lie in such extrema - but whats subtler is that some behaviours punish the excess and deficient equally, whereas others are fundamentally oblique in their consequences. We can treat it like a value-skewed tradeoff function - I call it *asymmetrical upshot*. 

Take confidence. Both overconfidence and underconfidence are suboptimal, yes. But I argue that society rewards the person who overestimates their capabilities far more than it even notices the person who underestimates theirs. If you're overconfident, they'll call you an arsehole. A prick. But at least you're playing the game. Most big swings aren't irreversible, type 1 decisions. They're not the kind of bet-the-company, burn-the-boat strategies that actually destroy you. They're recoverable failures with asymmetric upside. Swing and miss, then swing again. Each attempt expands your surface area for luck to find you. You only win if you bet. With underconfidence, you're not even folding bad hands— you never sat down at the table. You've optimised for a different kind of failure: the quiet kind where nothing ever happens to you. Zero doesn't compound. Overconfidence gets you in the room; underconfidence keeps you invisible. 

Curiosity: Too much can lead to chronic distraction, but too little is intellectual death by a thousand cuts. The world becomes smaller each day until you're trapped in the same thoughts, same conversations, same dead-end routines. 

Exercise: overtraining causes injury, undertraining invites gradual decay. The asymmetry? injury heals, decay spreads. The latter kills you slowly, quietly, and completely. 

Vulnerability: Too much vulnerability opens you up to hurt. Too little and you're isolated. The person who risks emotional exposure might get burned, but they're also in the running for genuine connection. Like with confidence, at least they've put themselves on the table, in the arena, playing the game, facing the dragon. The guarded person believes only in self-preservation; they're actually choosing the certainty of shallow interpersonal relationships and accumulated loneliness. Getting hurt heals. Never truly connecting doesn't. I'd like to think that's sort of what Tennyson meant by "better to have loved and lost, than never to have loved at all".

Asking for help: Too often and you're needy, reliant, embarrassing. Too little keeps you stupid. The person who asks for help risks appearing incompetent in that moment. The person who never asks spends years reinventing wheels, solving the solved, and struggling with issues others could fix in minutes. Pride is expensive. Ignorance is bankruptcy.

Then there's choice architecture, (reverse asymmetric upshot). We're sold the myth that more options beget better outcomes, that keeping doors open creates exponentiates possibilities. The counternarrative warns of analysis paralysis and decision fatigue. But here's what both miss: constrained choice often outperforms abundant choice. Feeling like you have too few options is frequently better than drowning in too many, despite what our choice-maximising culture preaches. Regret increases proportionally with number of choices available. The distribution becomes more diffuse, with most of it's mass concentrated around the option(s) we did not consider, the path(s) we did not take. 

There are of course domains which are balanced, whether neither side wins outright. Blood sugar doesn't care about your philosophy: too high gives you diabetes, too low puts you in a coma. When an org structure is too rigid it kills innovation and adaptability, too loose yields chaos and decision paralysis. True symmetries exist, but they're rarer than we think. 


I'd like to reframe how we think about optimisation. We could reflexively seek balance. Or we could ask which side of the mean punishes us harder? The answer can change everything about how we should behave. Of course, this depends  on the value judgments we assign to these outcomes. But isn't that the point? Asymmetrical upshot forces us to be explicit about what we're actually optimising for. Find the balance isn't bad advice, but you can always skew the crossbow a little. The upshot takes care of itself.
