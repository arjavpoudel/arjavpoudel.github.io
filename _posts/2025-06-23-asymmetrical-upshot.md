The conventional wisdom says extremes are universally bad. Find the golden mean, they say. But I think there's a subtler point here: True in the binary sense—most benefits don't lie in the extrema, but while some behaviours punish excess and deficiency equally, others are fundamentally lopsided in their consequences. It's like a value-skewed tradeoff function that challenges our assumptions about behavioural balance. I call it asymmetrical upshot. 

Take confidence. Both overconfidence and underconfidence are suboptimal, yes. But society rewards the person who overestimates their capabilities far more than it notices the person who underestimates theirs. Overconfidence gets you in the room; underconfidence leaves you invisible. 

If you're overconfident, they'll call you an arsehole. A prick. But at least you're playing the game.Most of life's big swings aren't irreversible, type 1 decisions. They're not the kind of bet-the-company, burn-the-boat strategies that actually destroy you. They're recoverable failures with asymmetric upside. Swing and miss, then swing again. Each attempt expands your surface area for luck to find you. Because again, you can only win if you bet. With underconfidence, you're not even folding bad hands— you never sat down at the table. You've optimised for a different kind of failure: the quiet kind where nothing ever happens to you. Zero doesn't compound. 

Curiosity: Too much can lead to chronic distraction, but too little is intellectual death by a thousand cuts. The world becomes smaller each day until you're trapped in the same thoughts, same conversations, same dead-end routines. At least the chronically curious person, is still alive in their mind, even if they're exhausting.

Exercise: overtraining causes injury, undertraining invites gradual decay. The asymmetry? injury heals, decay spreads. The latter kills you slowly, quietly, and completely. 

Vulnerability: Too much vulnerability can hurt — temporarily. Too little and you're isolated — permanently. The person who risks emotional exposure might get burned, but they're also in the running for connection, intimacy and authentic relationships. Like with confidence, at least they're on the table, in the arena, playing the game, facing the dragon. The guarded person thinks they're protecting themselves, but they're actually choosing the certainty of shallow relationships and accumulated loneliness. Getting hurt heals. Never truly connecting doesn't. I'd like to think that's sort of what Tennyson meant by "better to have loved and lost, than never to have loved at all".

Asking for help: Too often and you're needy, reliant, embarrassing. Too little keeps you stupid. The person who asks for help risks appearing incompetent in that moment. The person who never asks spends years reinventing wheels, solving the solved, and struggling with issues others could fix in minutes. Your pride will intellectually bankrupt you.

Of course there are domains which are balanced, whether neither side wins outright. Blood sugar doesn't care about your philosophy: too high gives you diabetes, too low puts you in a coma. When an org structure is too rigid it kills innovation and adaptability, too loose yields chaos and decision paralysis. True symmetries exist, but they're rarer than we think. 

Then there's choice architecture, (reverse asymmetric upshot). We're sold the myth that more options equal better outcomes, that keeping doors open creates exponential possibilities. The counternarrative warns of analysis paralysis and decision fatigue. But here's what both miss: constrained choice often outperforms abundant choice. Feeling like you have too few options is frequently better than drowning in too many, despite what our choice-maximising culture preaches. 

Regret increases proportionally with number of choices available. The distribution becomes more diffuse, with most of it's mass concentrated around the option(s) we did not consider, the path(s) we did not take. 

I'd like to reframe how we think about optimisation. Instead of reflexively seeking balance, we should ask which side of the mean punishes us harder? Sometimes the answer changes everything about how we should behave. Of course, this depends  on the value judgments we assign to these outcomes. But isn't that the point? Asymmetrical upshot forces us to be explicit about what we're actually optimising for. Find the balance isn't bad advice, but you can always skew the crossbow a little. The upshot takes care of itself.
