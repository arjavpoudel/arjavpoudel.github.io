Some interactions leave you more you. Others cost you a version of yourself. Or, as I like to call it:

Performative interaction: a setting where I'm compelled to lean into an amplified version of myself. Socially effortful, identity-amplifying, potentially fun, often high-octane -but equally depleting.

Restorative interaction: grounding, identity-affirming--even when unplanned. Leaves me ready to return to any task with a sense of renewal. 

Of course, my nervous system already knows the difference. I could then try and get clever --indexing the kinds of conversations that recharge, and optimizing for more, more, more. John won't shut up about his work problems. Lucy's hugs are underrated.  Helen couldn't organise a drama-less brunch if her botox depended on it. Coffee with Mark always leaves me calmer. The human response is simple: more Mark, more Lucy, less John and Helen. But as it stands, this is expensive trial-and-error. How many rounds of brunch roulette with different flavours of the same exhausting human must I endure before realising it's not the people, it's the pattern?

I like to imagine a partial two-player matrix. Each party can experience the interaction as either performative or restorative. That gives us 3 possible outcomes:

### 1. Restorative--Restorative

A pair of friends catching up. A couple of programmers hacking together a codebase in flow. The 2 person kayak trip you've been waiting all summer for. Possibly the Pareto-optimal corner of the matrix.

### 2. Performative--Restorative

So common it feels trite. One person is venting, seeking clarity, or simply unloading. The other is listening, validating, and (perhaps unwittingly) performing emotional labour. If the listener is resourced and aligned, they may _also_ feel restored--feeling useful can itself be restorative. But if the interaction is unexpected, one-sided, or recurring, it can leave the listener drained.

### 3. Performative--Performative

Each person is managing impression, narrative, posture. Often neither is truly seen. Ping Pong between the personas. 

Therapy:  where the client may be performing a self-image even in their most vulnerable setting, while the therapist performs neutrality.
ChatGPT:  where you may feel restored short-term by the lack of judgment--but subtly depleted long-term by the lack of resistance or co-authorship. 
Social Media: where interaction is _always_ spectated, thus always performative.

Here, affective yield is typically low. Nothing lands. So why even allow it to exist? 

**Incentives**. LinkedIn, social platforms, even polite conversation nudge us toward performance. Systems thrive on clarity. Metrics. Measurables. And anyone from the business world will tell you that what gets measured, gets managed. A social Goodhart's Law: when validation becomes the proxy for connection, we optimise for the wrong signal.

Performative interactions aren't entirely avoidable. They're not even inherently bad. Public speaking, flirting, pitching - high-octane stuff. Maybe necessary, given your goals.  The danger is the **default**, the baseline. If your social fabric is mostly performative,the strain adds up. You start to wonder: Why am I exhausted after a day with no visible stressors? Masks get heavy, especially when nobody else can know you're wearing one. 

So I'm not talking about eliminating performance, simply positing that  If your base state is fed by restorative ties, performative stretches are easier to recover from. Like lifting heavy between long rests.

And  interactional state spaces aren't static.  A transition function exists. A first date may start performative, warm into restoration, then veer back again. The transition itself is data-rich: who yields, who opens, who stays in mask? Some shifts signal resonance. Others, retreat.

There may even be attractor states. Certain dyads fall into restorative rhythm quickly and often. Others get stuck in performative ruts. And while the interaction feels mutual, the subjective experience rarely is. One party leaves wearing a glow, the other, a grimace. So taking an average to gauge quality of interaction is lossy. Perhaps the harmonic mean--an F1-score for 2 person interaction-- is a better model for this. 

Things to ask yourself:

- What percentage of my interactions are restorative? Is it enough?
- Do I mistake stimulation for nourishment?
- Are there structural incentives in my life nudging me toward constant performance?


Naming things won't fix them. But it helps. Just a lens I've found clarifying - and often rather freeing.
